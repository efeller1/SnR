**The publication's main point:** performance is about 20% of an allocator's decision (**4LEG**, **TR**). ODD is the top reason allocators pass, cited by 86% of them (**AIMA**). Most of what decides the allocation is the firm, not the returns.

## 0. Decide before going to market

Coming to market before these are settled is one of the five things allocators hold against a manager after a meeting (**AFTER**).

- [ ] **Regulatory framework and licences.** Own registration (SEC/state RIA or exempt reporting adviser) or a regulatory host or platform (**AFTER**, **ODD**).
- [ ] **Vehicle.** A fund, managed accounts (SMAs) or both, and which comes first (**AFTER**, **SMA**).
- [ ] **Domicile and base.** Where Perception and any fund are located, and what that means for licences and investors (**AFTER**).
- [ ] **Realistic timeline.** IMAs and ISDAs take months, and an ISDA can take six to twelve. Allocators check whatever launch date you say out loud (**AFTER**). Perception's long equity book may not need ISDAs; confirm.
- [ ] **Runway for the raise.** First engagement to institutional allocation takes 18–24 months, so plan for double what you expect (**2JOB**). An allocation needs 50–100 meetings (**4LEG**, **HOUR**, **25Y**).
- [ ] **Capacity, stated honestly.** Don't claim $1bn scalability because you think allocators want to hear it (**25Y**). An under-followed small and mid-cap strategy is probably capacity-constrained. Work out the number and say it. Capacity-constrained managers with high Sharpes are exactly who intermediaries like Vibe look for (**CAPINTRO**, **2JOB**).

## 1. Business plan and economics

Business acumen is one of the four things allocators assess, alongside performance, transparency and communication (**4LEG**).

- [ ] **Breakeven on management fees alone**, with no performance fees assumed (**4LEG**, **AFTER**). The AIMA survey puts average breakeven at about $82.9m AUM (**AIMA**). Allocators expect a properly structured fund to need $40–50m as a minimum (**ODD**).
- [ ] **Plan for surviving to breakeven**: cash, how long it lasts, and what you cut if it runs low (**AFTER**).
- [ ] **What the business looks like at $50m, $100m, $250m and $500m**: who you hire at each step (**AFTER**, **4LEG**).
- [ ] **What a flat year does to the business** (**4LEG**).
- [ ] **Your own money in the fund.** Allocators ask how much of your net worth is invested. "I can't afford to" doesn't answer it (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **Founder share class terms**, ready the day the first ticket lands (**ODD**).
- [ ] **The terms that matter on an SMA** before you sign one. See §6 (**SMA**).

## 2. People and governance

- [ ] **Separate running the business from running the portfolio (CEO/COO vs. CIO).** This comes up in allocator feedback more than anything else (**AFTER**, **2JOB**, **25Y**). Compliance and administration should be handled by someone other than the PM. Outsourced administration and compliance are the usual answer at this stage (**TR**).
- [ ] **Who does what**: where the buck stops, who oversees whom, who decides when you disagree (**AFTER**).
- [ ] **Named successor who can already trade the book** (**ODD**). Consider raising key-person risk yourself in the first meeting; one allocator said nobody had done it in 15 years (**SAME**).
- [ ] **Board or committee charters** (purpose, members, meeting frequency, how decisions are made), plus minutes and board packs. Allocators ask for the last four meetings, so start meeting now (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **An ops person who answers diligence requests**, not the CIO (**ODD**).

## 3. Track record

- [ ] **Know your level on the track record ladder** (**TR**):
  1. Backtest or simulated returns. Buys research conversations, not allocations.
  2. Returns from a prior employer. Needs attribution and portability cleared, preferably in writing.
  3. Your own money, live. Needs independent verification.
  4. Verified returns on a regulated platform.
  5. Audited fund returns (an independent administrator calculates NAV and an auditor signs off).
- [ ] **Move up the ladder.** If Perception's record is mainly the SnR screen and the Dual Momentum backtest, it is level 1. The next step is a live book with statements someone else can verify (**TR**).
- [ ] **Track record in Excel**, with daily returns available. For SMAs, show it gross of fees and net of trading costs (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **Backtest caveats written down**: survivorship, look-ahead, attention data history. The README already lists them. "I have never seen a bad backtest," so get ahead of the doubt (**TR**, **2JOB**).
- [ ] **Your drawdown story**: what you lost, why, whether the strategy was expected to lose there, and what changed. "We've never had a down month" is not the answer allocators want (**AFTER**).
- [ ] **Numbers that match everywhere.** Allocators check audited figures against marketing figures (**TR**).

## 4. Pitch and positioning

- [ ] **A two-sentence description no one else could use.** Avoid "uncorrelated, risk-adjusted returns" (**SAME**).
- [ ] **A simple account of how decisions get made**: the order of steps, the decision points and who does what. It should be specific enough that someone could prove it wrong (**AFTER**, **SAME**). For Perception: the universe, then the three filters, then the discretionary review, sizing and exit.
- [ ] **An edge an investment committee member who isn't a specialist could explain.** The allocator has to re-sell you to their committee (**4LEG**).
- [ ] **What you believe that others in your space don't**, and why you started the fund (**SAME**, **TR**).
- [ ] **Weaknesses named before you're asked**, with what you're doing about each (**SAME**, **4LEG**).
- [ ] **A fund deck and a strategy deck.** This is the minimum for first meetings. Don't send a fact sheet in place of the track record (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **Readable materials**: white space, a real author's voice, no black backgrounds, nothing that reads as written by ChatGPT or Claude (**DDQ**, **LEAK**).

## 5. Pick your investors

- [ ] **One sentence describing the investor most likely to write your next cheque at your current stage.** Count how many of your last ten meetings fit it (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Qualify every prospect on four tests**: eligible to invest, has capital to allocate now, fits a current mandate, and the person you meet can move the decision (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Check before you accept a meeting**: investor type, whether they invest directly or advise, current mandate, whether they back emerging managers, and what they want from the meeting (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Ask "Have you ever done day one?"** in the past tense, and when they last did (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Check ticket size against your fund size.** A £20m minimum ticket can rule out a £30m fund because the investor would own too much of it (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Separate people investing their own money from people investing someone else's.** The first can be your first cheque; the second may stay warm for 18 months and never lead (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Get into prime broker capital introduction.** Allocators finding managers through personal networks fell from 65% to 40%, and through cap intro rose from 22% to 30% (**AIMA**).
- [ ] **Avoid the names every prime broker is already showing.** Intermediaries look for managers outside London and New York with hard capacity limits (**CAPINTRO**).
- [ ] **Be ready to be checked by intermediaries**: regulated, through KYC, with references and counterparties that check out (**CAPINTRO**).

## 6. Managed accounts (SMAs)

Most large tickets now come through SMAs, not the fund (**ODD**, **SMA**).

- [ ] **Size floor.** The average SMA minimum is about $73m. For equity long/short, below about $30m rarely pays and $50m or more is the target (**SMA**).
- [ ] **Agree each of these in writing before signing** (**SMA**):
  - [ ] Termination notice period. This is your main defence against a fast exit.
  - [ ] How an exit runs: who liquidates the book and who bears the slippage.
  - [ ] What the allocator may do with your position-level data, and who they can share it with.
  - [ ] Who can change the investment guidelines, and on what timeline.
  - [ ] How trades are allocated across accounts, and how fairness is shown.
  - [ ] Most-favoured-nation terms. Your first cheap account sets the price for every later one.
  - [ ] Reporting scope, and how new reporting requests get handled.
  - [ ] Valuation of illiquid positions.
  - [ ] Tax and domicile treatment.
  - [ ] Whether the mandate changes your registration or reporting obligations.
  - [ ] **Rights to use the track record**, secured before you start.
- [ ] **Risk guidelines written into the IMA**: net and gross exposure, gross market value, concentration, sector limits and the drawdown at which they would terminate (**SMA**).
- [ ] **Fee economics beyond the headline rate**: how often the performance fee crystallises, cost pass-throughs, and the financing benefit the allocator gets from the structure (**SMA**).
- [ ] **Decide whether near-real-time position transparency harms a concentrated or reverse-engineerable book** (**SMA**).
- [ ] **Don't take too many custom SMAs too early.** The work grows much faster than the number of accounts (**SMA**).
- [ ] **Template IMA** reviewed by counsel (**ODD**).

## 7. Operational due diligence pack

The full list is in [`perception_capital_odd.md`](perception_capital_odd.md). The **DDQ** issue adds the following.

**Firm data** (**DDQ**)
- [ ] Firm AUM now, one year ago and two years ago, with an explanation if it is flat or falling.
- [ ] Headcount, split into full-time and part-time.
- [ ] Number of transactions by instrument type, per day, month and year.
- [ ] Every service provider, with the date you appointed them.
- [ ] Ownership, and investor concentration in dollars and as a % of firm AUM.
- [ ] Your own investment in the fund.

**Insurance at both firm and fund level** (**DDQ**, **ODD**)
- [ ] Broker cover, E&O, D&O and cyber, each with its coverage amount. "Deferring it to $500m AUM defers the allocation with it."

**Documents across the nine areas the DDQ covers** (**DDQ**)
- [ ] Governance and ownership structure
- [ ] Employees
- [ ] Fund structure and terms (for SMA-only managers this mostly falls away)
- [ ] Compliance and regulatory monitoring
- [ ] Risk management at firm and fund level
- [ ] Investment process and due diligence
- [ ] Operations, valuation and cash controls
- [ ] IT, cybersecurity and business resilience
- [ ] Oversight of service providers

**Detail managers tend to underestimate** (**DDQ**)
- [ ] **Conflicts of interest is at least five policies**: personal dealing, MNPI, gifts and entertainment, outside directorships and expert networks.
- [ ] **Alternative data policy**, covering web scraping and whether any of it could count as MNPI. This applies directly to Perception: the SnR screen collects Reddit, GDELT news counts, X and Yahoo data. Write down sources, terms-of-service compliance and the MNPI review.
- [ ] **Operations procedures as ten separate processes** covering the full life of a trade: idea → execution → matching → reconciliation → NAV sign-off.
- [ ] **IT and cybersecurity, 19 or more items.** Allocators skip managers with no annual pen test and no phishing test. A Google Workspace on default settings is a quick fail, so harden it (enforced 2FA, access control, device management).

**Proof that the policies are followed** (allocators ask for more than 24 items) (**DDQ**)
- [ ] Trade error policy **and the errors log**
- [ ] Log of investment guideline breaches
- [ ] Pen-test policy **and the latest external test results**
- [ ] Compliance training **and staff sign-off records**
- [ ] Disaster recovery plan **and the latest test results, with the report that went to senior management**
- [ ] Board or committee minutes and packs from the last four meetings
- [ ] Five years of audited financial statements **for the firm** (not only the fund)
- [ ] Three years of regulatory exam findings with the fix plan and which items are open or closed
- [ ] The administrator's latest internal controls report (e.g. SOC 1)

**Writing the DDQ** (**DDQ**)
- [ ] **Write your own documents.** Ten specific, current documents beat fifty generic ones. Templates bought in different years will contradict each other, and allocators fail managers on contradictions more often than on gaps.
- [ ] **Keep answers short.** For example, on AML: "Outsourced to [provider]; enhanced checks on [triggers]." Don't paste in the policy or the offering memorandum's legal language.
- [ ] **Answer N/A sections briefly, and keep them few.** Deleting them looks evasive.
- [ ] **Never write "available upon request."** If they asked, send it.
- [ ] **Update the DDQ every time AUM or staff change**, which is most quarters. Find one number in it that is no longer true and fix it.

## 8. Operations and controls (from **ODD**)

- [ ] Live trade reconciliation, not next-day.
- [ ] Order limits enforced twice, in your order system and at the broker. Sanctioned names blocked at the broker.
- [ ] E&O insurance of $5–10m a year, plus cyber.
- [ ] Two-factor login on everything, separate work phone and laptop, pen tests and phishing tests.
- [ ] Background checks on every employee. Pre-cleared references.
- [ ] Service providers in place: administrator, auditor, prime broker or custodian, counsel, compliance.

## 9. Speed and follow-up

Allocators treat how fast you respond as a test, whether or not they say so (**DDQ**, **ODD**, **LEAK**).

- [ ] **Standard items go out the same day or the next**: decks, track record and fixed policies (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **Commit to a date for anything custom and meet it.** Say Thursday and send it Thursday (**DDQ**).
- [ ] **Target: 20 questions answered within an hour**, with documents attached (**ODD**).
- [ ] **Promised follow-ups sent within 48 hours**, and track how often you hit that. Fewer than half of the first meetings Cláudia saw had the promised follow-up arrive within 72 hours (**AI**, **LEAK**).
- [ ] **Something other than your memory tracks commitments.** Every call is captured and every promise becomes a dated task (**LEAK**).
- [ ] **Log new contacts the day they appear**, researched, with their colleagues attached (**LEAK**).
- [ ] **Know who has been watching you quietly**, by name (**LEAK**, **AI**).

## 10. CRM and the raise process

- [ ] **A CRM that isn't a spreadsheet.** Free tiers exist (**TR**). Structure it as investors × strategies × meetings rather than a sales funnel (**AI**, **LEAK**).
- [ ] **Record meetings with consent and keep the full transcript**, not only an AI summary (**AI**, **HOUR**).
- [ ] **Log every meeting**: the questions asked, where the investor showed interest, what fell flat, and the exact words they used for objections (**HOUR**, **AI**).
- [ ] **Run the eight-question audit of your raise** (**AI**):
  1. When did you last have a meaningful conversation with each of your top ten target investors?
  2. What share of first meetings led to a second, by investor type?
  3. What exact objection did each investor who passed give?
  4. How often did promised documents arrive within 48 hours?
  5. Does your newsletter start from what investors have been asking, or from a blank page?
  6. How much content came out of your last podcast or appearance?
  7. Can you name the investors who have watched you for a year without engaging?
  8. How long does it take to map every investor relationship?
- [ ] **A written context file for your AI tools**: strategy, voice, phrases you use and avoid. Ask the AI to criticise drafts. You are responsible for anything AI helps write, including SEC marketing rule compliance (**AI**).
- [ ] **Build the system before hiring for investor relations.** Two IR and content hires cost £220–330k a year; the tools cost under £1,600 (**AI**, **LEAK**).

## 11. Meetings

- [ ] **Research the person and the firm beforehand**, in about 15 minutes (**HOUR**).
- [ ] **Open with their agenda**: what brought them here, what they're looking for now, how much time they have, and what would make the meeting useful (**HOUR**).
- [ ] **Talk 30% of the time and listen 70%** (**HOUR**). Cláudia presents for only 17.5% of her own calls (**CAPINTRO**).
- [ ] **Go in pairs** where you can: one person presents, the other watches the room. Debrief afterwards (**HOUR**).
- [ ] **Leave every first meeting knowing five things**: their process, who is already in their portfolio, recent allocations, what they need from you next, and their concerns (**HOUR**).
- [ ] **Ask about their decision process**: who is involved, who owns the final yes, how often the investment committee meets, and who runs investment and operational diligence (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Close with**: "Is this a good fit for what you're looking for right now? If so, what are the exact steps, and what would you like to see from us?" (**WRONG**).
- [ ] **Aim the first meeting at getting a second one**, not a cheque (**HOUR**).
- [ ] **Treat every investor as a 20-year relationship**, including those who pass. People change jobs (**2JOB**).

## 12. Communication and visibility

- [ ] **Write a monthly letter or fact sheet now**, as if 50 investors read it. It builds a record of how you think (**TR**).
- [ ] **Show your thinking, not only returns.** A short note after a major market event, or a risk nobody is talking about (**SAME**, **4LEG**).
- [ ] **Write more during drawdowns.** Never stop reporting to manager databases because the numbers are bad; a gap in reporting signals the drawdown anyway (**2JOB**, **ODD**).
- [ ] **LinkedIn profile** (**LURK**):
  - [ ] Headline with role, strategy and location (e.g. "Emerging hedge fund manager · US small/mid-cap growth · [city]").
  - [ ] About section: what you run, where your capital comes from, and how you think about risk, liquidity and time horizon. No performance figures or promises.
  - [ ] Experience written like the front page of a DD pack.
  - [ ] Featured section with 3–6 pieces to read.
  - [ ] Linked firm page, headshot, custom URL and contact details.
- [ ] **Post once or twice a week on LinkedIn and write one long piece a month**, made from work you've already done (**LURK**).
- [ ] **An email list you own** (Substack or your own newsletter). LinkedIn's audience is rented (**LURK**).
- [ ] **Judge success by the right people coming closer**, not impressions. Allocators read in silence for months (**LURK**, **2JOB**).
- [ ] **Sort inbound** into priority (allocators, family offices), defer (peers, media) and decline (pitches, poor fits) (**LURK**).
- [ ] **Have compliance review public content**, so no performance figures or fund solicitation appear (**LURK**, **AI**).

## 13. Skip or put off

- [ ] A prestige office. Nobody cared about Mayfair (**ODD**).
- [ ] Preparing for on-site visits. Many allocators never visit (**ODD**).
- [ ] Marketing AI use. Only 8% of allocators expect it, and 34% judge on pedigree and opportunity (**AIMA**).
- [ ] An expensive tier-one prime broker early, unless there's a reason. Allocators will ask what the catch is (**DDQ**).
- [ ] IR hires before the system and the AUM support them (**AI**).

---

## Suggested order for Perception

1. **Now:** §0 decisions, the business plan with management-fee breakeven, a two-sentence pitch, a CRM, the monthly letter and the LinkedIn profile. This is the cheap groundwork.
2. **Before first allocator meetings:** a fund and strategy deck, a track record in Excel with daily returns, a written process, references and a target-investor sentence.
3. **Before the DDQ arrives:** your own DDQ, the policies (including the alternative data policy), insurance, a hardened Google Workspace, a pen test, a phishing test and a succession plan. Start the logs and records that prove each policy is followed.
4. **Before the first large ticket:** live reconciliation, two sets of order limits, founder share class terms, a template IMA and your SMA term positions.
